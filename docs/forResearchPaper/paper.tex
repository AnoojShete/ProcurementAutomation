\begin{abstract}
Many procure-to-pay systems post an invoice without human review once its
extraction confidence passes a fixed threshold, but the error rate this admits is
seldom measured. It is also unclear whether checks against the organization's own
records would let more invoices skip review at the same error rate. We study both
questions on a seeded synthetic procure-to-pay world of 100 vendors, 1,649
purchase orders and 3,000 invoices, 29.8 percent of which carry one injected
business error, read with the extraction code of a deployed platform. We compare
the platform's confidence rule, a rule that accepts when every record check
passes, and risk-controlled gates that set their threshold with Learn-then-Test
from document evidence alone or from document and record evidence, over 20
vendor-disjoint splits. The deployed rule accepts 41.2 percent of invoices, and
7.2 percent of those are wrong. Record evidence improves ranking, raising the area
under the ROC curve from 0.735 to 0.945, but no gate specified in advance
certifies more than 2.6 percent of invoices at error budgets of 1, 2 or 5
percent, so our hypothesis of a five-point coverage gain is not supported. Most
top-ranked errors are resubmissions under a new invoice number, which the
production duplicate rule misses. A duplicate check that ignores the number,
defined after this diagnosis, certifies 21.0 and 37.0 percent of invoices at the 2
and 5 percent budgets on three new seeds, but exceeds the error budget in up to
20 percent of splits. All data are synthetic.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
An automated procure-to-pay (P2P) pipeline reads each incoming invoice, links it
to a vendor and a purchase order (PO), and then either posts it or sends it to a
person. Posting without review is a \emph{selective prediction} decision: the
system trades coverage, the share of documents handled automatically, against
the error rate among the documents it accepts \cite{elyaniv2010,geifman2017}.
Work on document extraction usually reports field accuracy on benchmarks such as
SROIE \cite{huang2019sroie} or LayoutLMv3 \cite{huang2022layoutlmv3}. A posting gate is judged differently:
what counts is how many accepted invoices are wrong, and an invoice can be wrong even
when every field was read correctly. It may be a resubmission, carry changed bank
details, or bill more than was ordered.

Learn-then-Test (LTT) \cite{angelopoulos2025ltt} chooses a threshold so that the
error rate among accepted items stays below a target with high probability.
Recent work brings calibrated confidence and conformal guarantees to
straight-through processing \cite{jin2026plv,rombach2026},
but scores each field on what the document itself
contains. Commercial P2P tools do match invoices against vendor masters and
POs \cite{rossum2026rules,rossum2026mdh,sapariba2026}, with hand-set tolerances
and no published error rates.

It accepts an invoice when a weighted extraction confidence is at least 0.8 and
no review flag is raised. We ask four research questions (RQs):
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1.} What error rate does this fixed rule admit on an invoice
stream that contains business errors?
\item \textbf{RQ2.} Does evidence from the organization's records let a
risk-controlled gate accept more invoices at the same error budget? Our
hypothesis (H1), fixed before any experiment, was a
gain of at least 5 percentage points over a document-only gate at a 2\% budget.
\item \textbf{RQ3.} How much more calibration data does per-field risk control
need than whole-document control?
\item \textbf{RQ4.} How much of the ranking signal survives when the records
are out of date?
\end{itemize}

The contributions are empirical. We do not propose a new risk-control method or
a new extractor. (1)~We measure the error rate of the deployed rule, and of a
rule that trusts every record check, on a P2P benchmark with known ground truth.
(2)~We compare risk-controlled gates with and without record evidence over
vendor-disjoint splits, with an ablation per check and a stale-records test.
(3)~We explain why no gate specified in advance certifies a useful share, and
report a post hoc fix, tested on new seeds, together with what it needs before it can be trusted.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
\subsection{Selective Prediction and Risk Control}
Selective classification lets a model abstain to lower its risk. El-Yaniv and
Wiener describe the resulting risk--coverage trade-off for noise-free
models \cite{elyaniv2010}, and Geifman and El-Yaniv build a selective classifier
on top of a trained network so that a chosen risk level holds with high
probability \cite{geifman2017}. LTT treats the choice of a low-dimensional
parameter, such as a threshold, as a multiple-testing problem and gives
finite-sample, high-probability guarantees for any underlying
model \cite{angelopoulos2025ltt}. Conformal risk control instead bounds the
expected value of a loss that is monotone in the
parameter \cite{angelopoulos2024crc}. Selective risk is not monotone in the
threshold, so we use LTT. The guarantee holds whatever the quality of
the score; the coverage obtained depends on how well the score
separates correct from incorrect items, which RQ2 varies.

Learning to defer models the human who receives rejected
items \cite{madras2018,mozannar2020}; we treat review as a fixed, error-free
fallback. The platform's field confidences are hand-set,
and even learned confidences are often miscalibrated \cite{guo2017},
so we learn a probability model before thresholding. LTT assumes that calibration and deployment
data are exchangeable; weighted conformal prediction relaxes this under
covariate shift \cite{tibshirani2019}, but we do not apply it (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\subsection{Document Extraction and Its Benchmarks}
Layout-aware models read business documents
well \cite{huang2022layoutlmv3}, and optical character recognition (OCR) engines such as
PaddleOCR and Docling are widely used \cite{cui2025paddleocr,auer2024docling}.
The benchmarks measure reading. SROIE covers receipts \cite{huang2019sroie}; DocILE \cite{simsa2023docile} and
FATURA \cite{limam2023fatura} cover invoices. None of them ships the purchase
orders, vendor master or payment history an invoice belongs to, so none can say
whether posting it was right. Krieger \emph{et al.}\ show that extractors degrade
on rarely seen suppliers \cite{krieger2023}, which bears on unseen test vendors.

\subsection{Confidence for Straight-Through Processing}
HYCEDIS scores extracted key--value pairs with a conformal predictor and an
anomaly detector \cite{nguyen2022hycedis}; Rombach and Mehdiyev build
entity-level conformal sets for receipts \cite{rombach2026}; ConfBench measures how well
vision--language model confidence tracks extraction accuracy \cite{roy2026confbench}.
The closest work is that of Jin
\emph{et al.}~\cite{jin2026plv}, who fuse perception, layout and validation
features with gradient boosting and set per-field LTT thresholds on DocILE,
FATURA and VRDU. Their validators are document-internal and add little signal on
invoices, and their decisions are per field.

\subsection{Procurement Practice and the Gap}
Most AI deployments in procurement sit in the P2P
cycle \cite{guida2023,spreitzenbarth2024}, and three-way matching is standard in commercial
tools \cite{rossum2026rules,rossum2026mdh,sapariba2026} (Table~\ref{tab:compare}).
Within the literature we searched, no
study (i)~measures the error admitted by a confidence-threshold gate on invoices
with business errors, or (ii)~tests whether enterprise records
change what a risk-controlled gate can certify for the whole
document. Practice already uses these checks as hard rules; what has not been
measured is their value inside a gate with an error guarantee.

\section{Problem Formulation}\label{sec:problem}
Each invoice $i$ has document evidence $D_i$ (extracted fields, field
confidences, text quality, classification confidence, internal consistency
checks), record evidence $E_i$ (results of checks against the vendor master,
open POs and earlier invoices), and a label $Y_i\in\{0,1\}$. We set $Y_i=1$ if
and only if the vendor, total, currency and invoice number were read correctly
\emph{and} no business error was injected. These four fields decide who is
paid, how much and against which document; a misread date or PO number does not by itself
make the payment wrong. A gate is a score $s(D,E)\in[0,1]$ and a threshold
$\lambda$; the invoice is accepted without review if and only if $s\ge\lambda$.
Coverage and selective risk are
\begin{align}
\Phi(\lambda) &= \Pr\!\left[s(D,E)\ge\lambda\right],\label{eq:cov}\\
\mathcal{R}(\lambda) &= \Pr\!\left[Y=0 \,\middle|\, s(D,E)\ge\lambda\right].\label{eq:risk}
\end{align}
For an error budget $\alpha$ and a confidence parameter $\delta$, we want the
threshold with the largest coverage such that
\begin{equation}
\Pr\!\left[\mathcal{R}(\hat\lambda)\le\alpha\right]\ \ge\ 1-\delta,
\label{eq:rc}
\end{equation}
where the probability is over the calibration sample. LTT tests the null
hypotheses $H_k:\mathcal{R}(\lambda_k)>\alpha$ on a grid
$\lambda_1>\lambda_2>\dots>\lambda_K$ ordered from strict to loose. If $n_k$
calibration invoices are accepted at $\lambda_k$ and $e_k$ of them are wrong,
the p-value is the binomial tail
\begin{equation}
p_k=\Pr\!\left[\mathrm{Bin}(n_k,\alpha)\le e_k\right].
\label{eq:pval}
\end{equation}
Fixed-sequence testing rejects $H_1,H_2,\dots$ while $p_k\le\delta$ and stops at
the first failure. This controls the family-wise error at $\delta$, so the
loosest rejected threshold satisfies \eqref{eq:rc} \cite{angelopoulos2025ltt}.
If $H_1$ is not rejected, nothing is accepted. Even with no errors ($e_k=0$),
rejection needs
\begin{equation}
n_k\ \ge\ \log\delta/\log(1-\alpha)
\label{eq:floor}
\end{equation}
accepted calibration invoices. Table~\ref{tab:floor} lists this floor for the
budgets we study, for a whole-document gate and for a per-component gate that
runs at $\alpha/5$. It matters in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:method}
\subsection{Deployed Rule and Extraction}\label{sec:deployed}
In the study, text PDFs are read with a text-layer parser and scans with
PaddleOCR 2.9.1 using the platform's settings and line
parsing \cite{cui2025paddleocr}; a TF-IDF and logistic-regression classifier assigns the document type and regular expressions
extract the fields. (The live pipeline also uses
Docling \cite{auer2024docling} and LayoutLMv3; neither is part of this study.) Each field $f$ has a confidence $c_f\in[0,1]$,
discounted by the quality $q\in[0,1]$ of the text it was read from. The
document score is a weighted mean:
\begin{equation}
s_f = c_f\,q,\quad
S=\frac{\sum_{f\in F} w_f\, s_f}{\sum_{f\in F} w_f},\quad
\text{review if } S<\theta=0.8,
\label{eq:conf}
\end{equation}
with $w_f$ = 0.30 (total), 0.20 (vendor name), 0.15 (classification), 0.15
(document number), 0.10 (date) and 0.10 (line items, left out when none are
found) \cite{more2026report}. The deployed rule also forces review when the
vendor name is missing or only resembles a known vendor, when the
document is not an invoice, when the PO match fails, when a
duplicate is found, or when bank details differ from the master. Confidence and
these flags together form baseline \sysB.

\subsection{Synthetic Procure-to-Pay World}
A seeded generator builds a vendor master of 100 vendors, each with a Goods and
Services Tax Identification Number (GSTIN), bank details and a currency (21 of the 100
vendors invoice in US dollars, the rest in Indian rupees, INR). Invoice volume
follows a Zipf-like weight $1/(1+i)^{0.8}$ over vendors ranked
$i=0,\dots,99$, so a few vendors send most invoices; the largest sends 350 of the
3,000. The generator then issues 1,649 POs and 3,000 invoices against them. Each
invoice carries at most one injected business error of eight types, listed with
their counts in Table~\ref{tab:data}. A resubmitted duplicate copies an earlier
invoice of the same vendor, moves its date by one to three days, and always
takes the vendor's next invoice number. Invoices are rendered as text PDFs or as
clean, mild or heavily degraded scans; ground truth is known by construction.

\subsection{Record Checks}
The checks reuse the platform's rules. Vendor matching compares the normalized
name with the master using the token-sort ratio of RapidFuzz 3.9.6; a score of 88 or more is a match and 70 to 87 a look-alike.
The three-way match compares the invoice's lines with the vendor's open POs and
holds the invoice when a unit price or the remaining balance is exceeded by more
than 1\%. Bank details, GSTIN and currency are compared with the master. Two
invoices $i,j$ of the same vendor are duplicates when
\begin{equation}
|a_i-a_j|\le 0.02\,a_j \ \wedge\ |d_i-d_j|\le 7\ \text{days}\ \wedge\
\mathrm{sim}(n_i,n_j)\ge 0.85,
\label{eq:dup}
\end{equation}
where $a$ is the amount, $d$ the date, $n$ the invoice number and $\mathrm{sim}$
a string-similarity ratio. All three limits are configurable business
rules \cite{more2026report}. Invoice numbers have the form INV-<vendor>-<counter>, such as INV-025-0003; between an injected duplicate and its original, $\mathrm{sim}$ has
median 0.83 and is below 0.85 for 50.3\% (74) of the 147 duplicates.
The exploratory check of Section~\ref{sec:posthoc} drops the last condition,
which equals setting that rule to zero. The checks add about 1--2~ms per invoice (measured in a Docker VM with 15 vCPUs and 7.7~GB on an Apple M5 Pro laptop).

\subsection{Gate Systems}\label{sec:systems}
We compare six systems:
\begin{itemize}
\item \sysB: the deployed rule of Section~\ref{sec:deployed}.
\item \sysBl: the deployed score $S$ of \eqref{eq:conf} with the same review
flags, but with its threshold set by LTT instead of fixed at 0.8.
\item \sysBtwo: accept when every record check passes (vendor matched and not a
look-alike, PO matched, no duplicate, and bank details, GSTIN and currency agree
with the master where present). It ignores extraction confidence.
\item \sysBone: a probability model of $\Pr[Y=1]$ on 19 document features,
thresholded with LTT.
\item \sysP: the same on the document features plus 16 record features (vendor,
PO, duplicate, bank, GSTIN, currency, vendor history).
\item \sysFC: one LTT gate per payment-critical component (vendor, total,
currency, number, business error), each at $\alpha/5$; an invoice is accepted
when all five accept. This mirrors per-field designs (RQ3) but does not certify
document-level risk: each gate bounds its component's error among the invoices
\emph{it} accepts, not among those all five accept.
\end{itemize}
The probability model is either a standardized logistic regression or histogram
gradient boosting (scikit-learn 1.9.1 \cite{pedregosa2011}; 300
iterations, learning rate 0.05, 15 leaves, L2 penalty 1.0). \sysFC{} uses logistic regression. The full feature
lists are in the code release.

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:setup}
Each of 20 random splits assigns vendors 50/25/25 to training, calibration and
test, so test vendors are never seen before. The model is fitted on training
vendors. Candidate thresholds come from quantiles of the in-sample training scores at 40 coverage levels, starting at 1.5 times the
floor \eqref{eq:floor} (as a share of the calibration set); the grid uses the calibration set only through its size, never through its
scores or labels. LTT then picks a threshold on the calibration vendors with
$\delta=0.1$. The plan fixed error budgets of 1\%, 2\% and 5\%; we added 10\%
after nothing passed at 5\%.

For each system we report means over splits of coverage \eqref{eq:cov} and risk
\eqref{eq:risk} on the test vendors, and the \emph{violation rate}, the share of
splits with test risk above $\alpha$, which should be at most $\delta$. Mean risk
is the mean over all 20 splits, a split that accepts nothing counting as zero risk, so it
understates the risk of a gate that accepts in few splits; we then also give
the risk over the splits that accept. A gate that accepts nothing in any split has its risk
shown as a dash. For the learned gates we also report the area under the ROC curve (AUROC) and under the risk--coverage
curve (AURC). We give the value in INR of wrongly accepted invoices, converting
US-dollar invoices at 83 INR per dollar.

Coverage differences between systems get a paired percentile bootstrap 95\%
confidence interval (CI) over splits, which describes variation across splits, not the sampling
uncertainty of the generator (the splits reuse the same 100 vendors). Violation rates
get exact Clopper--Pearson 95\% intervals, since a test set of about 750 invoices makes them
noisy even when the guarantee holds.

For RQ4 we make a share of the records stale (0 to 40\%): those vendors' bank
details change and their GSTIN goes missing, and the same share of POs is never
entered. The model is trained on equally stale records. One script writes all figures to a results file with the
generator version, seed and commit.

\subsection{Error Rate of the Deployed Rule}\label{sec:rq1}
\sysB{} accepts 41.2\% of invoices (standard deviation
1.2 points across splits), and 7.2\% of those are wrong, so about 3.0\% of all
invoices would be paid in error. The wrongly accepted invoices are worth INR
15.8 million (158.3 lakh) per test set, which holds about 750 invoices on
average. Trusting the record checks alone (\sysBtwo) covers more, 58.8\%, at
almost twice the risk (13.2\%, INR 39.3 million). \sysB{} already raises most of
these checks as review flags, so its errors pass the flags as
configured. Neither rule
meets a 2\% or 5\% budget (Table~\ref{tab:main}). \sysB{} does meet a 10\%
budget in every split (its highest split risk is 9.0\%). Over all 3,000 invoices, \sysB{}
wrongly accepts 93 of the 1,232 it accepts: 46\% carry a wrong GSTIN and 41\% are
renumbered duplicates. \sysBtwo{} wrongly accepts 248 of 1,766: 34\% have an
arithmetic error, 25\% a misread field, and 22\% are duplicates.

Tuning the threshold of the deployed score with LTT (\sysBl) gives
no coverage at either budget: it accepts nothing in all 20 splits (AUROC 0.851, AURC
0.171). Tuning $\theta$ alone therefore does not fix the rule, because the errors it
admits, wrong GSTINs and renumbered duplicates, are not ones that extraction
confidence can see.

\subsection{Value of Record Evidence}\label{sec:rq2}
Record evidence raises AUROC from 0.735 to 0.945 with gradient boosting and from
0.745 to 0.940 with logistic regression, and halves AURC (0.268 to 0.127). The
PO match carries most of this. Removing it lowers
the logistic AUROC from 0.940 to 0.833; removing bank details, the duplicate
check or the GSTIN check gives 0.898, 0.921 and 0.923, while vendor matching,
currency and history change it by at most 0.002. The three-way match runs only
for a matched vendor, so the PO features carry most of what vendor matching adds.

Better ranking did not become coverage at the planned budgets. \sysP{}
certifies nothing at 1\% and 2\%, and 2.6\% at 5\% (mean risk 0.6\% over all splits, 4.3\% over the
3 splits that accept; one violation),
accepting invoices in only those 3 of the 20 splits; \sysBone{} certifies
nothing. The paired gain of \sysP{} over \sysBone{} is $+0.0$ points at
$\alpha=2\%$ and $+2.6$ points (95\% CI $[0.0,5.9]$) at 5\%, so H1 is not
supported. The gain appears only at the unplanned 10\% budget: \sysP{} then covers 16.6\% at 2.4\% risk and a 5\% violation rate (1 of
20 splits; CI 0.1--24.9\%), a gain of $+16.6$ points (CI $[5.9,28.6]$) over
\sysBone{} (Fig.~\ref{fig:coverage}).

\subsection{Why the Gates Certify Little}\label{sec:why}
At a 2\% budget, LTT needs at least 114 accepted calibration invoices with
almost no errors (Table~\ref{tab:floor}). Among the top 16.2\% of calibration
invoices ranked by \sysP{} (mean over splits), the smallest slice that reaches this count,
3.8\% are wrong, so no threshold passes and LTT accepts nothing. Of the
86 wrong invoices in these slices (summed over splits), 67 (78\%) are renumbered resubmissions.
The duplicate rule
\eqref{eq:dup} needs the two invoice numbers to be at least 85\% similar, which
half of the new numbers are not (Section~\ref{sec:method}), and the PO still has
quantity left, so the three-way match passes too. The rest are overbilled
quantities (7), arithmetic errors (5), misread fields (4) and
overbilled prices (3). The limit lies mainly in one check, not in the model.

\subsection{Exploratory Number-Independent Duplicate Check}\label{sec:posthoc}
Adding a feature for the duplicate check without its number condition gives
\sysPdup. With gradient boosting it certifies 20.7\% of invoices at
$\alpha=2\%$ (risk 0.7\%) and 39.9\% at 5\% (risk 2.9\%), with AUROC 0.971. It accepts in 11 of 20 splits at 2\% (risk 1.2\% among them) and in 18 at 5\% (3.2\%). Its
violation rate is 15\% at both budgets (3 of 20 splits; CI 3.2--37.9\%) and 20\% at
the unplanned 10\% budget (4 of 20; CI 5.7--43.7\%). All three point estimates are above
$\delta=0.1$, although with 20 splits the intervals do not exclude it. The error
guarantee is therefore not met reliably. On correct invoices the check without the
number condition fires on 4.6\% (82 of 1,794); the generator creates some
legitimate repeat invoices with the same amount within seven days (26 of 2,105 clean invoices), so this false
alarm rate may be optimistic.

The gate was defined after we saw the diagnosis, on the same seed. We therefore
froze its definition (commit 1306b21) and ran it unchanged on 3 new
generator seeds, where it covers $21.0\pm10.3\%$ at $\alpha=2\%$ and
$37.0\pm7.0\%$ at 5\% (mean $\pm$ SD over the three seeds). Its violation rate on those seeds
is 20, 10 and 0\% at 2\% and 5, 10 and 10\% at 5\%. In a generator variant where 50\% of
duplicates keep the original number or get a one-character edit, its coverage on seed 7 is
20.3\% and 42.0\% (violation rate 15\% at both budgets).

Table~\ref{tab:failures} lists the largest groups of invoices that \sysPdup{}
still accepts wrongly at $\alpha=10\%$, the one budget with enough accepted invoices to show a pattern. Arithmetic errors on clean scans lead. They are
not missed by the extractor: its consistency check reports a mismatch on all 127 of them, but neither the
deployed rule nor the learned gate treats that flag as a reason to stop. Next come correct invoices whose total or
number was misread on a scan, and overbilled prices on clean scans. Renumbered duplicates still appear, mostly on
clean scans. In 38 such cases the amount matched the original in all, but the date was not
read at all in 26 and the numbers were less than 85\% similar in 17, so the rule did not pair those invoices.

\subsection{Per-Component Gates and Stale Records}\label{sec:rq34}
\sysFC{} accepts nothing at any budget. Table~\ref{tab:floor} explains why: at
$\alpha/5$, each component gate needs at least 575 error-free accepted
calibration invoices at a 2\% budget and 230 at 5\%, against about 750
calibration invoices in total, of which only 59.8\% are correct. Per-component
control therefore needs five times the calibration data of document-level
control at the same budget (RQ3), and still would not certify document-level
risk. A valid per-component design would score each
invoice by its lowest component probability and run one LTT on document-level
risk, keeping the whole-document floor.

With stale records (RQ4), the AUROC of \sysP{} falls steadily, from 0.940 to
0.899, 0.881, 0.864 and 0.838 as 5, 10, 20 and 40\% of the records go stale
(logistic regression; Fig.~\ref{fig:stale}). It stays above the document-only
0.745 at every level. The logistic gate's coverage is near zero at all levels,
so we repeat the test with gradient boosting at $\alpha=10\%$, where \sysP{}
covers 16.6\% on current records: its coverage is 20.1, 8.1, 9.8 and 5.1\% at 5, 10, 20 and 40\%
stale, and its violation rate 10, 0, 5 and 5\% (5\% on current records). Coverage falls by about two thirds at 40\% stale
records, but not monotonically; the means are noisy with 20 splits.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}
The invoices, records and error rates come from one generator and no real or
public invoice data were used, so absolute coverage and risk will not transfer to
another organization; the generator also decides how detectable each error is.
Every injected duplicate carries a new number, so the gain from the
number-independent check partly reflects that design choice; the variant above suggests it is not the whole gain. The share of
invoices with a business error, 29.8\%, is far above what most organizations
see, so coverage at a fixed budget would
be higher at a lower prevalence. The planned run on public
invoices with derived records has not been done. \sysPdup{} was chosen after inspecting results.

Invoices of one vendor are not independent, but the binomial test \eqref{eq:pval} treats them as
if they were; with one vendor sending 350 of 3,000 invoices, the effective
calibration size is smaller than the count. This may
explain the violation rates above $\delta$ of \sysPdup{}: 15\% at 2\% and 5\%, and 20\% at the
unplanned 10\% budget. No correction for this, such as cluster-aware bounds or
weighted calibration \cite{tibshirani2019}, was applied.

Each calibration set holds only 25 vendors, and we use a single $\delta$. Reviewers are treated as free
and always right \cite{mozannar2020}. Results depend on one extraction stack; the Docling and LayoutLMv3 paths were not exercised.
Seed 7 was re-run on 6 Nov 2026 after the OCR image was rebuilt; the earlier run (19 Oct) gave somewhat different
numbers, so absolute values depend on library versions.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
On a synthetic P2P benchmark, a hand-set confidence rule accepted 41.2\% of
invoices with 7.2\% of them wrong, above all three budgets we planned to
certify. Record evidence improved the ranking of invoices substantially, mostly
through the PO match, but no risk-controlled gate specified in advance certified
more than 2.6\% of invoices at budgets of 5\% or less, and the hypothesized gain
was not found. The main bottleneck was one rule: resubmitted invoices with a new
number pass the duplicate check. A check that ignores the number certified
21.0\% and 37.0\% at the 2\% and 5\% budgets on three new seeds, but it was found by looking at the
results and its error rate exceeded the budget in up to 20\% of splits.

Next we will use public invoices (DocILE or FATURA) with derived records,
cluster-aware bounds, and a model of reviewer cost.
